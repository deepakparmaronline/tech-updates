In a large, established company, SEO is less about writing a few pages and more about systems. The work is governance, templates, technical checks, dev tickets, reporting and getting many teams to agree. Keyword research and content still matter, but the biggest wins often come from fixing a template that affects thousands of pages.

## What Happened?

A user on r/SEO asked what the actual SEO work is in a big, already established company. The thread was about five days old, with 57 votes and 54 comments. We could see only the title. We did not read the comments, so we do not report what people with enterprise jobs said.

This is a useful question for anyone planning an SEO career or hiring for one. The work at a large firm looks different from freelance or small business SEO. We use public sources to describe it. Most of them are vendor or agency pages, so we mark them as such.

## Why People Are Talking About It

Most SEO advice is written for small sites. It talks about blog posts, keywords and backlinks. People who join a large company often find a different job.

At scale, a single change can affect millions of URLs. A wrong rule can hide a whole section from Google. Many teams own parts of the site, and each has its own goals. So SEO becomes a team sport.

Newcomers want to know what the day looks like before they accept a role. Others want to know if their small-site skills transfer. They do, but they are only part of the job.

## What Users Experienced

We could not read the thread, so this part relies on public descriptions of enterprise SEO. These are expert and vendor views, not proof for any one company.

[Semrush describes enterprise SEO](https://semrush.com/blog/enterprise-seo) as using many of the same techniques as normal SEO, tailored for enterprise-scale sites. It says the work requires coordination across multiple teams and buy-in from multiple stakeholders. It also says that standardized workflows, governance models and automation help manage SEO at scale, and that custom reporting for different stakeholders is often necessary.

Semrush also suggests creating templates, batch updates for large changes, regular audits, and working with subject matter experts inside the company.

Agency pages describe similar work, such as writing dev tickets, specifications and QA checks, and attending sprint planning. These are marketing pages from agencies, so they show what agencies sell. They are not a neutral survey.

## Why It Happens

Scale changes the job. A site with tens of thousands of pages cannot be edited one page at a time. Changes go through templates, rules and systems.

Process matters because many people touch the site. Engineering, product, content, legal, brand and regional teams may all have a say. Someone has to keep SEO rules clear and agreed. That is governance.

Technical risk is also higher. Google's [crawl budget guide](https://developers.google.com/crawling/docs/crawl-budget) says that crawl budget matters mainly for large sites with 1 million or more unique pages that change about weekly, and for sites with 10,000 or more pages that change very quickly. It also applies when many URLs show as "Discovered, currently not indexed" in Search Console. That is a verified statement from Google. It also says that if your site does not fit those cases, a current sitemap and regular index checks are enough.

So at a large firm, SEO also covers crawl and index control: managing the URL inventory, handling duplicate and parameter URLs, returning correct status codes, and keeping pages fast to load. Those are all points in Google's guide.

## Working Fixes

If you are in this role, or preparing for one, this is a realistic scope. It is our synthesis of the sources above, not an official job description.

1. **Learn the site's templates.** Know the main page types, such as category, product, article, location. Audit one example of each. Fixes at template level scale.
2. **Write clear requirements.** Turn SEO needs into short tickets with the problem, the page examples, the expected result and how to test it.
3. **Join the release process.** Check changes before launch. Redirects, canonicals, robots rules and metadata are common failure points.
4. **Set up regular audits.** Crawl the site on a schedule. Compare results to the last run. Flag new errors early.
5. **Manage the URL inventory.** Decide which URLs should be indexed. Control duplicates and parameters as Google's guide advises.
6. **Create content standards.** Provide brief templates, naming rules and linking rules so many authors stay consistent.
7. **Build reports for different readers.** Executives want revenue and risk. Engineers want exact URLs and fixes. Content teams want topics and gaps.
8. **Prioritize by impact.** Estimate pages affected, traffic at risk and effort. Argue for work in business terms.
9. **Work with experts.** Get input from product, legal and subject matter experts so content is accurate and trusted.
10. **Keep a change log.** Record releases and SEO changes so you can explain drops and gains later.

## What Doesn't Work

- **Treating it like small-site SEO.** Editing pages one by one does not scale.
- **Skipping stakeholder work.** A perfect audit does nothing if no team owns the fix. Vendor pages say that coordination and buy-in are core challenges.
- **Worrying about crawl budget when you do not need to.** Google says most sites do not need to read its large-site guide.
- **Leaving soft 404 pages in place.** Google's guide says soft 404 pages keep being crawled and waste crawl budget.
- **Sending long reports.** Busy teams skip them. Short, clear actions get done.
- **Launching changes without tests.** A wrong rule on a template can hit thousands of pages.

## Official Response

Google does not describe enterprise SEO jobs. Its crawl budget guide is the closest official source. It says very large or fast-changing sites should manage their URL inventory, return 404 or 410 for permanently removed pages, avoid soft 404 pages, and make pages efficient to load.

Google also says new pages can take several days to be noticed, and that same-day crawling is not expected for most sites.

Everything else in this article, including governance and ticket writing, comes from industry practice and vendor descriptions. Companies differ, so your own role may differ.

## Key Takeaways

- Large-company SEO is mostly systems, process and coordination.
- Template-level fixes can affect thousands of pages at once.
- Google says crawl budget matters mainly for sites with 1 million or more pages, or 10,000 or more pages that change daily.
- Governance, tickets, QA and reporting take up much of the time.
- Reports must match the reader: executives, engineers or content teams.
- Small-site skills still help, but they are only part of the job.
- Check each company's real scope before you accept a role.

## FAQ

### What does an SEO do at a big company?

Most of the work is governance, templates, technical audits, tickets, QA and reporting. It also includes content standards and working with many teams.

### Is enterprise SEO different from normal SEO?

It uses many of the same techniques but at larger scale. Semrush says it needs more coordination across teams and more stakeholder buy-in.

### Do large sites need to manage crawl budget?

Google says it matters mainly for sites with 1 million or more unique pages changing weekly, or 10,000 or more pages changing daily. Others usually do not need to.

### Do I need to know how to code?

Not always, but technical literacy helps. You must read templates, understand status codes and write clear tickets for developers.

### Why is progress slow at large companies?

Many teams must agree and release cycles are long. Vendor sources name coordination across stakeholders as a main challenge.

### Can small-site skills transfer?

Yes. Keyword research, content, and technical basics all apply. You will also need process, communication and data skills at scale.

### How should I prepare for an enterprise role?

Learn how to audit by template, write specifications, and report to different readers. Practice on a large site if you can.
