If you published a large batch of AI-written posts and your traffic fell, do three things first. Check the Manual actions report in Search Console. Stop publishing more of the same. Then sort every AI page into keep, improve, merge, or remove. Google does not ban AI content. It targets mass pages that add no value.

## What Happened?

A site owner on r/SEO asked how to recover SEO after publishing a lot of AI-generated blog posts. The thread was about six days old when we checked, with 49 votes and 118 comments. We could only see the title and a short snippet of the page. We did not read the comments, so this article does not repeat what commenters said.

The question is common, so here is the short version of the rules. Google's own guidance says generative AI can help with research and with adding structure to original content. It also says that using AI to generate many pages without adding value for users may violate its spam policy on scaled content abuse. You can read that in [Google's guidance on generative AI content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content). This is a verified fact from Google.

So the problem is not the tool. The problem is volume without value.

## Why People Are Talking About It

AI writing tools make it cheap to publish fifty or five hundred posts in a week. Many site owners did exactly that. Now some of them see traffic drops and want to know if they caused it.

Two things add to the worry. First, Google's spam rules now name scaled content abuse directly. Second, Google's raters guidelines also cover it. Google points raters to section 4.6.5 for scaled content abuse and section 4.6.6 for main content made with little effort, originality or added value. Google also states that these guidelines are not a guide to ranking and that rater scores do not directly affect ranking. They show what Google's systems aim to reward.

[Search Engine Land reported on the 2025 rater guideline update](https://searchengineland.com/google-quality-raters-content-ai-generated-454161). It said the update added a definition of generative AI and a focus on low-effort content made at scale. That is reliable tech news, but it describes rater guidance, not live ranking rules.

## What Users Experienced

We did not open the comments, so we cannot report what thread members said. Here is what industry sources describe as common signs. Treat these as reports, not proof.

- Traffic falls on a whole folder or template, for example only the blog or only comparison pages. One recovery guide from SEOJuice says a pattern tied to a template points to scaled content abuse or site reputation abuse. This is an expert opinion.
- Pages stay "Discovered, currently not indexed" or are indexed but get almost no impressions.
- A notice appears in Search Console. This is the clearest signal, and it is rare compared with quiet drops.

If none of these match, your drop may come from something else, such as a core update, a seasonal change, or a tracking issue. Check before you delete anything.

## Why It Happens

Google has two ways to act on spam. One is a manual action, where a human reviewer applies a penalty and you get a message. The other is automatic. Google's systems can lower pages without telling you.

Google's help page says that if a manual action affects your site, it notifies you in the Manual actions report and in the Search Console message center. You can read it in [Google's Manual actions report help](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9044175). That is a verified fact.

If you see no notice there, an industry guide from [Coalition Technologies](https://coalitiontechnologies.com/blog/reconsideration-request-get-help-writing-your-reconsideration-request) says the drop was algorithmic and a reconsideration request would do nothing. That is an expert view, but it matches how Google describes the process: reconsideration is for manual actions.

Why would bulk AI pages be at risk? Because they often repeat what already ranks, add no first-hand detail, and exist only because a keyword list said to make them. That fits Google's description of low value pages made at scale. This explanation is an inference from Google's wording, not a confirmed cause for any single site.

## Working Fixes

These steps follow Google's published guidance. Where a step is our advice, we say so.

1. **Check for a manual action.** Open Search Console and look at Security and Manual actions. If there is a notice, read the exact type and the list of affected pages.
2. **Stop the bulk publishing.** Do not add more pages until the cleanup is done. This is our advice.
3. **List every AI page.** Export all URLs from your CMS or sitemap. Add clicks and impressions from Search Console for the last 6 to 12 months.
4. **Sort each page into one of four groups.** Keep pages that already get traffic and are correct. Improve pages with real demand but thin content. Merge pages that answer the same question. Remove pages with no traffic, no links, and no reason to exist.
5. **Improve with real value.** Add first-hand experience, original examples, data you collected, clear author names, and checked facts. Google asks you to focus on accuracy, quality and relevance, especially when content is generated automatically.
6. **Remove the right way.** For pages you delete for good, return a 404 or 410 status code. Google's crawl budget guide says to do this for permanently removed pages. If a close replacement exists, use a 301 redirect to that page.
7. **If you have a manual action, fix all affected pages first.** Google says to fix the issue on all affected pages and to describe your fixes in the reconsideration request.
8. **Write a plain reconsideration request.** Say what was wrong, what you changed, and how many pages you removed, merged or rewrote. Keep it factual. Google says reviews can take several days or weeks.
9. **Track results monthly.** Look at clicks, not only impressions. Recovery from an algorithmic drop can take longer than a manual action review. No source we found gives a fixed time, so be wary of anyone who promises one.

## What Doesn't Work

- **Sending a reconsideration request with no manual action.** It does nothing, according to industry guidance.
- **Mass disavowing links in a panic.** One recovery guide lists this as a top mistake. Disavow files are for link problems, not thin content. This is an expert opinion.
- **Light edits.** Changing a few words, swapping the intro, or running text through a rewriter keeps the same low value. Google's concern is value for users.
- **Deleting everything.** Some AI-assisted pages may be good and already earn traffic. Removing them throws away useful work.
- **Hiding the cause.** If you bought a site that had spam before you owned it, Google says to mention that in your reconsideration request.
- **Adding disclaimers only.** Google says that sharing how content was made can give readers context. It does not fix weak content.

## Official Response

Google's position is steady and clear. Its guidance says AI can help with research and structure, and that mass pages without added value may break the scaled content abuse policy. It tells site owners to focus on accuracy, quality and relevance. It also says that how a page was made can be shared to help readers, and that ecommerce sites have extra rules in Merchant Center, such as IPTC metadata for AI-generated images.

For manual actions, Google says it will revoke the action if it finds your site no longer violates its spam policies. It does not publish a fixed timeline.

We did not find a Google statement about recovery speed after an algorithmic demotion. Any number you see for that comes from private studies or opinion.

## Key Takeaways

- AI content is not banned. Mass pages without value for users are the risk.
- Check the Manual actions report first. No notice means a reconsideration request will not help.
- Sort pages into keep, improve, merge and remove.
- Use 404 or 410 for pages you delete for good, and 301 redirects only where a close match exists.
- Add real experience, data and checked facts to the pages you keep.
- Stop bulk publishing until the cleanup is complete.
- Do not trust anyone who promises a recovery date.

## FAQ

### Does Google penalize AI-generated content?

Not for using AI alone. Google says AI can help with research and structure. It may act when many pages are made without adding value, which falls under scaled content abuse.

### How do I know if I have a manual action?

Open Search Console and check the Manual actions report and the message center. Google says it notifies you in both places. If nothing is listed, the drop is not a manual action.

### Should I delete all my AI blog posts?

No. Keep posts that are accurate and already earn traffic. Improve thin ones, merge duplicates, and remove pages with no value. Deleting everything wastes good work.

### Should I use a 404 or a 301 redirect?

Use 404 or 410 for pages removed for good. Use a 301 redirect only when a close replacement page exists. Google's crawl guide recommends 404 or 410 for permanent removals.

### How long does recovery take?

Google gives no fixed time. It says manual action reviews can take several days or weeks. For algorithmic drops, no official time exists, so avoid promises from any vendor.

### Do I need to disavow links after an AI content problem?

Usually not. Disavow is for link issues. An industry guide lists mass disavowing as a common mistake when the real problem is thin content. Fix content first.

### Can I keep using AI to write?

Yes, if each page adds real value. Use AI for research and structure, then add your own experience, data and fact checks. Share how you made the content when readers would wonder.
