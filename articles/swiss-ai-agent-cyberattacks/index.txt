19:T3221,<p>On November 26, 2026, Swiss public broadcaster SRF reported a new count from the Federal Office for Cyber Security: five recent cyberattack cases involving AI agents had been confirmed. BACS director Florian Schütz told SRF that four of the five attacks were successful and three affected operators classified as critical infrastructure. SRF&#39;s 07:30 News am Morgen briefing on November 26 carried the five-case update. Reuters later reported the same figures at 12:25 UTC (17:55 IST).</p>
<p>The report adds a clearer picture to the agency&#39;s earlier November 24 warning about AI agents being used in cyberattacks. It does not name the affected organisations or the attackers. The new figures show why security teams should treat automated vulnerability discovery as a current operational risk, while avoiding claims that every attack used a new or unknown exploit.</p>
<h2 id="news-summary">News Summary</h2><p>The key update is the confirmed count: five cases, four successful compromises and three involving critical infrastructure operators. The figures came from BACS director Florian Schütz in an interview reported by SRF on November 26. Reuters later reported the same figures in a separate report published at 12:25 UTC, or 17:55 IST.</p>
<p>BACS had already warned on November 24 that AI agents were being used in cyberattacks against organisations in Switzerland. The agency described systems that can search for vulnerabilities, select tools and carry out steps with less direct human input. The November 26 update gives a more specific number of cases and a clearer view of the organisations affected.</p>
<p>The public information remains limited. Neither SRF nor Reuters named the companies, described every intrusion in detail or attributed the activity to a specific group. The count is important, but it should not be turned into a claim that every organisation is facing the same attack method.</p>
<h2 id="what-happened">What Happened</h2><p>The Swiss Federal Office for Cyber Security, known as BACS, has been tracking cases in which attackers used AI agents as part of their operations. In the November 26 SRF interview, the agency&#39;s director said five cases had been confirmed in recent days. Four were successful, and three affected critical infrastructure operators.</p>
<p>The new figures are a follow-up to the agency&#39;s earlier public warning. In its November 24 <a href="https://www.bacs.admin.ch/en/26-ki-agenten-1-en">official notice</a>, BACS described AI agents that can search for vulnerable systems, identify weaknesses and continue through attack steps. The agency warned that these tools can lower the effort needed to find exposed systems and exploit known weaknesses.</p>
<p>The November 26 <a href="https://www.srf.ch/news/news-am-morgen-0730">SRF News am Morgen briefing</a> carried the five-case update. <a href="https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/ai-agents-used-five-cyber-attacks-switzerland-government-says-2026-11-26/">Reuters also reported the five cases</a>, confirming the same split between successful attacks and critical infrastructure cases.</p>
<p>The reporting does not say that the five attacks all happened on November 26. The director described cases from recent days. The important new development is that the agency has now publicly confirmed the number and the impact categories.</p>
<h2 id="why-it-matters">Why It Matters</h2><p>AI agents can make some parts of cyber operations faster. A tool that can search for exposed systems, test known weaknesses and decide what to try next may reduce the time an attacker needs to move from discovery to an attempted intrusion. That does not mean the agent is automatically capable of every part of a complex attack. It means the cost of repeated scanning and routine exploitation can fall.</p>
<p>The risk is serious for organisations that leave vulnerable services exposed to the internet. A system that has not been patched can become a target even if the company has not been singled out by a human attacker. Automated tools can search large address ranges and continue working after an initial discovery.</p>
<p>The effect on critical infrastructure deserves attention because these organisations support services that people rely on. A successful intrusion can affect operations, data, suppliers and public confidence. The reports do not say that all three operators suffered service outages, so the confirmed fact is that they were affected by attacks, not that every service stopped working.</p>
<p>The case also shows why defenders should focus on basic security controls. AI-enabled tools do not remove the need for attackers to find a weakness. Fast patching, strong authentication, limited exposure and monitoring can still prevent many common intrusion paths.</p>

<p>The BACS notice from November 24 describes AI agents that can perform multiple steps, including searching for vulnerabilities and attempting to exploit them. The agency&#39;s concern is not only that an AI model can write malicious code. It is that an agent can connect several actions into a workflow and continue testing systems with less manual input.</p>
<p>The public reporting does not establish that zero-day vulnerabilities were used. BACS&#39;s earlier notice discusses known weaknesses and exposed systems. This distinction matters because defenders should not assume the attacks require a previously unknown vulnerability. A known vulnerability that remains unpatched can be enough.</p>
<p>The five-case count should also not be confused with a global total. It is the number of cases BACS said it had confirmed in Switzerland, based on the reporting available on November 26.</p>
<h2 id="who-is-affected">Who Is Affected</h2><p>The most direct concern is for Swiss organisations that operate critical services or support them through suppliers. The reports said three affected cases involved critical infrastructure operators, but the organisations were not named. That means other companies should not try to guess which sectors or brands were involved.</p>
<p>Small and medium-sized businesses are also relevant. An exposed server, remote-access service or outdated application can be a target even when the organisation does not consider itself a high-value target. A smaller company may also provide software, maintenance or data services to a larger critical infrastructure operator.</p>
<p>Security teams should review their internet-facing assets and confirm that the people responsible for patching can act quickly. IT teams should know which systems are exposed, which vulnerabilities are urgent and how to restrict access while a patch is prepared.</p>
<p>Boards and business leaders should treat this as an operational risk, not only a technical issue. They need to know who owns incident response, how suppliers are assessed, how backups are tested and how the organisation will continue to operate if a system is compromised.</p>
<h2 id="what-happens-next">What Happens Next</h2><p>BACS is likely to continue sharing guidance and case information with affected organisations. The November 24 notice already advised defenders to pay attention to known vulnerabilities and internet-facing systems. The November 26 figures may lead more organisations to review their exposure and patching process.</p>
<p>The next useful public details would be the attack methods, the types of systems involved, the time between vulnerability disclosure and exploitation, and the controls that stopped or limited each incident. Those details would help defenders prioritise work without exposing sensitive information about the affected organisations.</p>
<p>Organisations should not wait for every detail to become public. Review external assets, remove services that do not need to be reachable from the internet, apply security updates quickly and make sure remote access uses strong authentication. Keep logs and alerts that can show unusual login attempts, unexpected processes and changes to critical systems.</p>
<p>Incident response plans should include a way to isolate affected devices, preserve evidence, restore from clean backups and communicate with customers and regulators. A plan that has never been tested may fail when staff are under pressure.</p>
<h2 id="what-we-know-and-do-not-know">What We Know and Do Not Know</h2><p><strong>What is known:</strong> BACS has confirmed five recent cases involving AI agents, according to the November 26 SRF report. Four attacks were successful, and three affected critical infrastructure operators. The agency had already issued an official warning on November 24 about AI agents being used to search for vulnerabilities and carry out attack steps.</p>
<p><strong>What is not known:</strong> The public reports do not name the affected organisations, identify the attackers, publish full forensic reports or describe every system involved. They do not prove that zero-day exploits were used. They also do not say that all affected services were disrupted.</p>
<p>The term AI agent can cover different tools and levels of autonomy. Some systems may automate scanning and routine actions, while others may require a person to approve important steps. The public reports do not provide enough technical detail to classify each case.</p>
<p>It is also important not to overstate the impact. The fact that three critical infrastructure operators were affected is significant. It does not mean that all three suffered a public outage or that every Swiss critical service is compromised.</p>
<p>The figures should be read as a confirmed update from the Swiss agency, not as a complete map of every AI-assisted attack taking place in the country.</p>

<li>BACS confirmed five recent AI-agent cyberattack cases in Switzerland, according to a November 26 SRF report.</li>
<li>Four attacks were successful, and three affected critical infrastructure operators.</li>
<li>The public reports do not name the victims or attackers.</li>
<li>The agency&#39;s earlier warning focused on exposed systems and known vulnerabilities.</li>
<li>Fast patching, strong authentication, reduced exposure and tested incident response remain important.</li>
